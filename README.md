# What does it cost to get one validated, stable, new material?

This repository is the replication package for the paper of that title. It holds the cost model, every
parameter with its source, the first-hand benchmark data behind some of those parameters, and the tests.

**Research question.** Across three computational discovery pipelines, what does it cost to obtain
(1) one material that DFT confirms is stable, (2) one material that is then made in the lab as the target
phase, and (3) one such material that is genuinely new to science?

| Pipeline | Stages |
|---|---|
| A | ion-exchange pool → drop structures already in Materials Project → DFT on every candidate → synthesis |
| B | ion-exchange pool → drop known → ML interatomic potential (MLIP, CHGNet) relax-and-classify → DFT on the ones it passes → synthesis |
| C | generative model (MatterGen) → drop known → MLIP → DFT → synthesis |

The ion-exchange pool is generated by data-mined ionic substitution on Materials Project prototypes: the
probabilistic substitution model of Hautier, Fischer, Ehrlacher, Jain & Ceder (2011, *Inorg. Chem.* 50(2), 656-663)
[`hautier2011substitution`], in the implementation Szymanski & Bartel (2025) benchmark [`szymanski2025baselines`].

Every pipeline pays to generate its candidates. For the ion-exchange pool that is about $0.02 per batch of 500.
Checking whether a structure is already in Materials Project (MP) is a database lookup and is not charged.

**Status: stage 2** (tornado, Monte Carlo with named scenarios, kill-condition check, marginal returns,
break-evens). Stage-2 sections of this README are generated by `scripts/stage2.py`; full tables are in
`results/stage2.md`.

### Level 1 (a): an MLIP pre-filter cuts DFT cost per stable material by about 40%, not several-fold

B costs $19.05 per DFT-confirmed stable material against A's $31.48, a ratio of 1.65×, so B is 39% cheaper.
The filter saves money because DFT then runs on a pool that is 15.2% stable instead of 9.2%. The relaxations
themselves cost $0.0009 per robust prediction -- 0.004% of B's level-1 compute -- and generating the pool a
further $0.0010. (An earlier version of this line said "about 3 cents", which was the ion-exchange GENERATION
cost per batch, two lines up, attached to the wrong quantity. It was then corrected to $0.0019, which is
generation AND relaxation together, so it was still charging generation to the relaxations; the two halves are
now separate.)

**The previous draft said 4×. The correction is worth reporting, because others will make the same error.** That
draft took CHGNet's classification rates from Matbench Discovery (recall 0.76, false-positive rate 0.13) and
applied them to the ion-exchange pool. Those rates predict that 37% of what passes the filter is DFT-stable.
Szymanski & Bartel measured the same filter on the same kind of pool and found 15.2%. Using the measured purity
cuts the filter's level-1 benefit from 4.0× to 1.65×, more than halving it (`--filter matbench` reproduces the
4.0×). Matbench rates are measured on the WBM set, with its own construction, stability reference and 15.5%
stable fraction. They do not transfer to a pool built differently.

### Level 1 (b): a generative pool is about 4× worse than ion exchange, because of purity at the DFT gate

C costs $76.61 per DFT-confirmed stable material against B's $19.05, 4.0×. Both pipelines run the same CHGNet →
DFT funnel. The difference is what reaches DFT. After the CHGNet filter, 15.2% of ion-exchange candidates are
DFT-stable, against 3.8% of MatterGen's. DFT spend per stable hit is (DFT cost) / purity, so the ratio is about
15.2 / 3.8 = 4.0. Generation cost plays no part: MatterGen's GPU time is 0.5% of C's compute spend. Against the
unfiltered DFT-only pipeline A (ion-exchange pool 9.2% stable), C is 2.4× worse. This is Szymanski & Bartel's
conclusion expressed in dollars. It is not an independent reproduction, because the model takes their
measurements as inputs.

**One of the two numbers behind this finding is read from a figure.** The paper's text prints only "3.8% to 8.8%"
for MatterGen and CDVAE together. Which value is MatterGen's comes from Supplementary Fig. 13, where MatterGen
sits at about 3.75% and CDVAE at about 8.8%. The labels are unambiguous and the text pins the value, so the
plot-reading error is small (±0.3 points). Sampling error on 300 structures is larger. **The 4× is sensitive to
this value:**

This grid sweeps the record's own range, where the high endpoint 6.98% is the Wilson 95% upper bound
on MatterGen's own 3.8%. The paper's Limitation 3 sweeps a different thing — the full 3.8% to 8.8%
range the source prints for two models without assigning endpoints, i.e. "what if the MatterGen
point were the other end of the printed range" — and so reports 4.02× to 1.75× rather than the
4.0× to 2.2× below. Both are legitimate; they answer different questions, and neither is the other's
correction.

| C / B at level 1 | MatterGen purity 2.28% (lowest consistent) | 3.8% (base) | 6.98% (high, Wilson on MatterGen's own point) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ion exchange purity 15.2% | 6.7× | 4.0× | 2.2× |
| ion exchange purity 11.6% / 19.7% | | 3.1× / 5.2× | 1.7× / 2.9× |

MatterGen purity below 2.28% (= 3.0% stable × recall 0.76) is impossible: the filter would have to pass more than
everything. The model refuses those inputs. **The direction is robust:** across the whole range, C costs more
than both B and A (at best for C, $41.89 against A's $31.48). The size, about 4×, is not robust. It is 2–7×
depending on a figure-read value and its sampling error.

### Level 2: at the base DFT cost, pipeline choice changes total cost per validated material by 17%; at the top of the DFT span, by 2.56×

At the base DFT cost (54.5 core-h per candidate) the pipelines are 1.17× apart at level 2 ($1,019 / $983 /
$1,149); at 1,000 core-h per candidate they are 2.56× apart. The near-equality is structural. Level-2 cost per
material is

`(1 − w) × compute $ per DFT-confirmed stable / success  +  recipes × $ per attempt / success`

and the second term, synthesis ($928 at base), contains no quantity that depends on the pipeline. Every
DFT-stable verdict goes to synthesis. The wrong-verdict share w, the success rate, the recipes per target and the
cost per attempt are the same however the candidate was found. `tests/test_structure.py` checks this across every
parameter level and choice: level-2 cost differs between pipelines only through the compute term.

**This holds across the DFT cost span, but the spread widens at the expensive end.** DFT cost per candidate runs
from 3 to 1,000 core-hours (see "DFT cost"):

<!-- generated:dftspan:begin -->
| DFT core-h per candidate | Level 1: A / B / C | Level 2: A / B / C | Level 2 spread | Synthesis share of level 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.975 (our measurement) | $1.72 / $1.04 / $4.56 | $933 / $931 / $941 | 1.01× | 99%–100% |
| 54.54 (geometric midpoint) | $31.48 / $19.05 / $76.61 | $1,019 / $983 / $1,149 | 1.17× | 81%–94% |
| 1000 (MP 2012, upper) | $577 / $349 / $1,398 | $2,590 / $1,934 / $4,953 | 2.56× | 19%–48% |

Synthesis is the majority of level-2 cost at every point in the span. Even at 1,000 core-hours, the level-2 spread
(2.56×) is well below the level-1 spreads.

**Read 1.17× as a lower bound on the level-2 pipeline difference.** It assumes that synthesis success does not
depend on how a candidate was found. That assumption has a likely direction. MLIPs are trained on Materials
Project data, and Materials Project is mostly materials that have actually been made. So an MLIP pre-filter
plausibly favours candidates that are *more* synthesisable than an unfiltered pool. If so, B's and C's success
exceeds A's and the true spread is wider. See "Primary threat to validity".

### Named finding: CHGNet's discovery acceleration factor is 1.65× on a realistic ion-exchange pool, against 3.36× on WBM

The discovery acceleration factor (DAF) is the ratio of the filter's precision to the pool's prevalence: how many
times more often a filter-passed candidate is stable than a random one. Matbench Discovery defines it exactly so
("Formally the DAF is the ratio of the precision to the prevalence"). On Szymanski & Bartel's pools it is the
measured post-filter purity divided by the measured unfiltered stable fraction. That is the same quantity on both
sides, so the comparison needs no assumption about which classifier rate transfers.

| Pool | Stable fraction | Purity after CHGNet | DAF | DAF if Matbench rates held at this prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WBM (Matbench Discovery, reported) | 15.3% |  | 3.36× |  |
| ion-exchange pool (Szymanski & Bartel) | 9.2% | 15.2% | **1.65×** | 4.01× |
| MatterGen pool (Szymanski & Bartel) | 3.0% | 3.8% | **1.27×** | 5.05× |

On the ion-exchange pool CHGNet's enrichment is 1.65×. On MatterGen's pool it is
1.27×: the filter barely enriches at all. DAF depends on prevalence (its ceiling is
1 / prevalence), so the last column applies Matbench's recall and false-positive rate at each pool's own
prevalence. That makes the like-for-like comparison 1.65× against
4.01×, a larger gap than against the reported
3.36×.

**Mechanism (rests on an assumption).** If Matbench's recall (0.76) transfers to these pools,
the measured purity implies a false-positive rate of 0.43 on the ion-exchange pool
(0.59 on MatterGen's), against 0.13 on WBM. The other attribution is
that Matbench's false-positive rate transfers and recall collapses. That would need recall of
0.23 and
0.17, which are implausible. The DAF result above does not
depend on this attribution.

**Why the gap: two explanations, one unresolved.**

1. *Distribution shift is weak as an explanation.* WBM was "generated via chemical similarity-based elemental
   substitution of MP source structures" over five rounds (Matbench Discovery, section 2.1.2). That is the same
   construction as Szymanski & Bartel's data-driven ion exchange. If anything, Matbench's unique-prototype subset
   removes structures whose protostructure matches MP, while ion exchange only reuses MP prototypes (0% novel
   prototypes). So the WBM test set is not the more conservative pool.
2. *The stability criteria differ, an unresolved competing explanation.* Matbench labels a structure stable if
   it is "on or below the convex hull of the MP training set" (MP v2022.10.28). It explicitly rejects using the
   joint MP + WBM hull, and it notes that only about 20k of the about 33k WBM structures below the MP hull remain
   on the joint hull. Szymanski & Bartel compute ΔE_d ≤ 0 against phase diagrams that "included all entries from
   MP (as of June 2025) as well as the entries generated from each AI model or baseline approach". That is a
   newer MP and a hull that also contains the other generated candidates, close to the joint-hull formulation
   Matbench rejected. The two criteria agree on the threshold (on or below the hull) but not on the hull. A
   stricter hull lowers both prevalence and the share of passed candidates that count as stable, so part of the
   gap may be definitional rather than a weakness of the filter. Public data can resolve this: recompute CHGNet's
   DAF on WBM against the joint MP + WBM hull. Until then the DAF gap is a measured fact, but its cause is open.

## Primary threat to validity

The central claim rests on one assumption: **synthesis success does not depend on the pipeline.** No source in our
parameter tables measures whether synthesis success depends on how a candidate was found. The obvious follow-up
study is to attempt matched sets of MLIP-filtered and unfiltered DFT-stable candidates and compare success rates.

To size the threat, the model has a switch, `synth.success_multiplier_mlip_filtered` (default 1). It scales
synthesis success for pipelines B and C only. Raising it divides their level-2 cost by the same factor. The base
case reports the value needed to separate the pipelines:

<!-- generated:multiplier:begin -->
| Level-2 spread | Multiplier needed | Success in B and C | Success in A |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5× | 1.45 | 42% | 29% |
| 2× | 1.93 | 56% | 29% |

For pipeline choice to double the cost per validated material, an MLIP pre-filter would have to roughly double synthesis success, from 29% to 56%.
<!-- generated:multiplier:end -->
That is below A-Lab's best reading, 63.2% unconditional, so the required effect is within the range campaigns
have reported -- though no study measures success by pipeline.
This number is a result in itself: it says how strong a synthesisability effect the follow-up study would need
to find for pipeline choice to matter at level 2.

## Cost basis: whose cost, and F&A

Institutional F&A (indirect cost) is the largest single accounting choice in the synthesis cost. Synthesis is
81%–94% of level 2 at base, so this choice moves the headline dollar figure, though not the spread. The base case prints
all three variants:

<!-- generated:costbasis:begin -->
| Basis | $ per attempt | of which indirect cost | Level 2: A / B / C | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `bench`: direct costs only | $191 | $0.00 | $744 / $708 / $874 | 1.23× |
| `funder`, fees outside the indirect-cost base (**default**) | $271 | $80.05 | $1,019 / $983 / $1,149 | 1.17× |
| `funder`, fees inside the indirect-cost base | $271 | $80.05 | $1,019 / $983 / $1,149 | 1.17× |

Between `bench` and fees-inside-base the choice moves level 2 by 37% ($744 against $1,019 for A), but not the spread.
<!-- generated:costbasis:end -->

- **Default `funder`.** The question is the resource cost of a validated material. F&A pays for real space,
  utilities and administration that a funder bears. `bench` is the cost a lab's own budget sees, and it
  understates the economic cost.
- **Service-centre fees are outside the F&A base by default.** XRD and furnace fees are internal recharge rates,
  and federal rules require such rates to "normally consist of [the service's] direct costs and an allocable share
  of all indirect costs" (2 CFR 200.468(b)(2)). Charging institutional F&A on top would count that overhead twice.
  Practice varies between institutions, and a reviewer may reject this choice.
  `choices.fa_base_includes_service_fees: true` no longer changes anything, because since the 2026-10-11 move to
  `indirect_base: total_direct` the base already contains the fees: the attempt stays at $271 and level 2 at
  $1,019 / $983 / $1,149. The switch bites only together with `indirect_base: mtdc`, where it gives $262.
- **The F&A base is total direct costs, including service-centre fees and tuition.** It is $191 per attempt,
  giving $80.05 of indirect cost; the MTDC-like variant that excludes fees and tuition gives $21.91 and is
  carried as a scenario (`indirect_base: mtdc`). The rate is 42%, the average *effective* indirect cost recovery rate,
  what NIH actually pays, from Azoulay, Gross & Sampat (2025, NBER WP 33627) [`azoulay2025icr`]. The same paper puts
  the average *negotiated* rate at 58%; that is the ceiling institutions may charge, not what is paid, and applying
  it would overstate indirect cost by about a third. The parameter's high end is 45% and its low end 25% (the
  paper: effective rates are "generally between 25% and 45%"); the 58% negotiated average is carried as a
  separate illustration, not as the top of the effective range. The data are NIH indirect-cost records for 354
  grantee institutions (universities, hospitals and research institutes), not a census of US universities, and
  the effective rate is measured on total direct costs rather than MTDC, so 42% leans low for our narrower base.

## Level 3: lower bounds only

Level 3 divides the level-2 spend by the number of validated materials that are also new to science. The evidence
for that share is one disputed count. Leeman et al. re-analysed A-Lab's claimed new materials and found **0 of
36** new. The model's base value is the Wilson 95% upper bound on 0 of 36, 9.6%, which is the largest share the
data allow. Each level-3 figure is therefore a **lower bound**. At the observed share of 0, the cost is unbounded:
no number of attempts yields a new material. The tests check that the model returns ∞ there rather than failing.

<!-- generated:level3:begin -->
At base, the lower bounds are about $12,000 for each of A, B and C (A ≥ $12,181, B ≥ $11,753, C ≥ $13,146).
<!-- generated:level3:end -->
Levels 1 and 2 rest on measurements. Level 3 rests on one contested count, so it is reported as a discussion and
sensitivity item, never as a headline figure.

## The MLIP filter: measured purity, and the recall it can't pin

**Base: measured purity.** Szymanski & Bartel ran CHGNet on MP-novel structures from each method and sent the ones
it predicted stable (ΔE_d ≤ 0) to DFT. They measured the DFT-stable share of what passed: 15.2% for ion exchange
(in the text) and 3.8% for MatterGen (from the figure, see Level 1 (b)). The Matbench path is kept as a selectable
alternative (`--filter matbench`).

**Recall can't be pinned from the paper.** Recall would follow from purity, the pool's stable fraction and the
filter's pass fraction, but v2 of Szymanski & Bartel does not report the pass fraction. v1 reports one (48.8% for
ion exchange) for an earlier dataset that includes non-novel structures. v1's own figures imply a recall of
0.518 × 0.488 / 0.191 = **1.32**, which is impossible, so they don't describe one population and can't be used.
Recall therefore stays at Matbench's 0.76, graded *proxy* with its range widened to 0.40–0.95. Purity and recall
now come from different sources and cannot both be exactly right. Under measured purity, recall barely moves the
cost per hit, because DFT spend per stable verdict is (DFT cost) / purity. Recall sets how many of a fixed pool's
true stables are lost.

## DFT cost

DFT cost per candidate is modelled as a **span from 3 to 1,000 core-hours**, not a defended point value:

- **Low, 2.98 core-hours: our measurement.** It is the cost per SCF iteration at 18 atoms × 11.76 SCF iterations
  per ionic step × 12 ionic steps. The run was Quantum ESPRESSO 6.7, PBE, GBRV ultrasoft pseudopotentials, 4 MPI
  ranks on an ARM64 laptop under WSL2 (`data/dft_benchmark/`). The same fit gives 3.59 core-hours at 20 atoms;
  the raw 20-atom measurement is 3.77, and the two are not the same basis.
- **High, 1,000 core-hours: Materials Project, about 2012.** It is 1–5 calculations per material at about 200
  CPU-hours each, with 10–20% of jobs failing and rerun, on decade-old hardware (NERSC user-day slides). The
  per-calculation figure (200) and the totals-based figure (about 100 per compound) are kept as cross-check
  records.
- **Base, 54.5 core-hours: the geometric midpoint.** This is a convention, not a measurement. Stage 2 should
  sample the span log-uniformly.

### Marginal returns: where the next dollar of improvement goes

<!-- generated:returns:begin -->
Return = % fall in cost per validated material per 1% PROPORTIONAL improvement in the lever: the log-elasticity d ln(cost) / d ln(lever) at the evaluation point, signed so that a positive number means the improvement lowers cost. It is scale-free, so levers in different units compare directly.

| Group | Lever | A base | B base | C base | A MC median (5–95%) | B MC median (5–95%) | C MC median (5–95%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tied, synthesis | synthesis success rate | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) |
| tied, synthesis | recipes per target | 0.911 | 0.944 | 0.808 | 0.975 (0.605–0.999) | 0.985 (0.714–0.999) | 0.944 (0.396–0.997) |
| tied, synthesis | cost per synthesis attempt | 0.911 | 0.944 | 0.808 | 0.975 (0.605–0.999) | 0.985 (0.714–0.999) | 0.944 (0.396–0.997) |
| compute | compute price | 0.089 | 0.056 | 0.192 | 0.025 (0.001–0.395) | 0.015 (0.001–0.286) | 0.056 (0.003–0.604) |
| compute | DFT cost per candidate | 0.089 | 0.056 | 0.191 | 0.025 (0.001–0.395) | 0.015 (0.001–0.286) | 0.055 (0.003–0.604) |
| compute | MLIP precision (filter purity) | 0.000 | 0.056 | 0.191 | 0.000 (0.000–0.000) | 0.015 (0.001–0.286) | 0.055 (0.003–0.604) |

**The synthesis levers are tied at unit elasticity; they are not ranked.** Level-2 synthesis cost is recipes × cost per attempt / success, so each of the three has elasticity one by construction. Success is exactly 1 because it also divides the compute term. Recipes and cost per attempt equal the synthesis share of level-2 cost (0.911 for A, 0.944 for B, 0.808 for C). The gap between them is the compute share, not a difference in leverage. `tests/test_stage2.py` asserts this structure.

**The finding is the magnitude gap.** A 1% improvement on the synthesis side lowers cost per validated material by at least 0.91% (A), 0.94% (B), 0.81% (C). The best computational lever lowers it by at most 0.089% (A), 0.056% (B), 0.192% (C). That is a ratio of 10× (A), 17× (B), 4× (C) at the base point, and 39× (A), 64× (B), 17× (C) at the Monte Carlo medians. Proportionally, synthesis is worth 4–64 times compute, depending on the pipeline and the evaluation point.

### Kill-condition check: do the pipelines separate under uncertainty?

**Verdict** (an ordering survives if it holds in at least 95% of paired draws; it is material if the two differ by more than 10% in at least 50% of draws).

- Level 1: B vs A **separates**: B is cheaper than A in 99.4% of draws and they differ by more than 10% in 98%; C vs B **separates**: B is cheaper than C in 99.7% of draws and they differ by more than 10% in 99.6%.
- Level 2: B vs A **ordered but immaterial**: B is cheaper in 99.4% of draws, but they differ by more than 10% in only 10%; C vs B **ordered but immaterial**: B is cheaper in 99.7% of draws, but they differ by more than 10% in only 34%.
- Level 3: B vs A **ordered but immaterial**: B is cheaper in 99.4% of draws, but they differ by more than 10% in only 10%; C vs B **no ordering survives**: C is cheaper than B in 48.5% of draws.

The expectation going in (level 1 separates, level 2 does not) is confirmed in a specific sense. At level 2 the ordering B < A still holds draw by draw, because the synthesis term is shared and the compute term is not, but the difference is immaterial. **The marginal distributions overlap heavily at every level, level 1 included (B–A overlap 90%).** The absolute cost is dominated by uncertainty that all pipelines share: the 3–1,000 core-h DFT span at level 1, and the synthesis worldview at level 2. So the pipelines cannot be told apart by comparing their absolute cost ranges. Only the paired comparison, the same world under each pipeline, separates them, and only at level 1.

## The equations, in plain English

Symbols: N = MP-novel candidates in the batch, n = atoms per cell, π = fraction of candidates that are truly
stable by DFT. Compute is counted in core-hours (and GPU-hours for GPU generation) and turned into dollars only
at the end (equation 8).

**1. MLIP cost per candidate.**
`cost = k · n · [(1 − q) · s̄ + q · s_cap] / 3600` core-hours.
An MLIP relaxation is a sequence of ionic steps. Each step costs about the same per atom, k core-seconds, and
our benchmark shows that per-step time grows linearly with cell size (log-log slope ≈ 1), so one step on an
n-atom cell costs k·n. The number of steps does not depend on size: our relaxations took 18 to 81 steps. We use
the mean, s̄, because the expected cost of a linear quantity is its cost at the mean; stage 2 will sample the full
distribution. A fraction q of relaxations never converge and run until the step cap s_cap. These are charged the
full capped cost and produce no candidate.

**2. DFT cost per candidate.**
A value from the 3–1,000 core-hour span (see "DFT cost"). The alternative, `--dft first_hand`, uses
`cost = a · (n / n_ref)^b · m · i / 3600`. Here a·(n/n_ref)^b is our measured core-seconds per SCF iteration at
size n, m is SCF iterations per ionic step, and i is ionic steps per relaxation. A relaxation runs i geometry
steps, each step needs m electronic iterations, and each iteration costs more on bigger cells.

**3. What the MLIP filter lets through.**
Base (measured purity P): `pass fraction = π · R / P`, and the DFT pass rate equals P.
The filter passes every candidate it thinks is stable. A share R (recall) of the truly stable ones get through,
and P is the share of what gets through that is truly stable. So the number passed is (number truly stable
caught) / P. Alternative (`--filter matbench`): `pass fraction = π · R + (1 − π) · FPR`, with precision
`π · R / pass fraction`. Here FPR is the share of unstable candidates wrongly passed. Both rates come from
Matbench's confusion matrix. At Matbench's own π = 15.5% this returns the published precision (a test checks
this). If the inputs imply a pass fraction above 1, the model refuses to run, because the inputs contradict
each other.

**4. How stages chain.**
To get N candidates that are new to MP, a method must generate N / (MP-novel share) structures. That is 72.4%
for ion exchange and 91.8% for MatterGen, so generation is charged on N / share structures. Szymanski & Bartel's
stability rates are measured on MP-novel structures, so this is the population they describe. Each later stage
takes in what the previous stage let out: `surviving = entering × pass rate`, so the count at the end is the
number generated times the product of all pass rates. Each stage's cost is (candidates entering) × (cost per
candidate), because you pay for every candidate you process, including those that fail.

**5. Wrong DFT verdicts.**
`correct verdicts = DFT-stable verdicts × (1 − w)`.
A share w of "stable" DFT verdicts are wrong. The proxy for w is the share that flip to unstable under a higher
level of theory (r2SCAN or SCAN): 13–34%. A wrong verdict costs twice. Its DFT run, the most expensive
computational stage, yields nothing. And we cannot tell which verdicts are wrong, so every DFT-stable candidate
goes on to synthesis, and the w share of those attempts are wasted too. Level 1 therefore divides by correct
verdicts, not all verdicts. The synthesis success rate is the campaign's *unconditional* rate, which already
embeds that campaign's computational failures, so (1 - w) is applied to the compute term only and these failures
are not counted twice.

**6. Synthesis cost.**
`direct = XRD + furnace + labour + crucible / firings + precursor grams × $/g + other consumables`;
`per attempt = direct + tuition + F&A rate × (direct + tuition)` (F&A on the funder basis only; the base is
total direct costs, service-centre fees and tuition included, because the 42% effective rate is defined by its
source over total direct costs -- the narrowed base is the `indirect_base: mtdc` scenario); `per target = recipes per target × per attempt`.
No published dollar cost per solid-state synthesis exists, so it is built bottom-up:

- **XRD and furnace:** facility fees per sample and per furnace-hour.
- **Labour:** a graduate trainee's stipend and fringe, divided by the 10–20 compositions per week a trainee makes
  (the Liverpool estimate), so the person and the throughput match.
- **Crucible:** its price divided by the firings it survives.
- **Precursors:** a stoichiometric mix priced from catalogue list prices for our six compositions.
- **Other consumables:** ethanol, weighing dishes, gloves and milling balls.
- **F&A (indirect cost), on the `funder` basis only:** 42% (the effective indirect cost recovery rate) of labour, fringe, crucibles, precursors and other
  consumables. XRD and furnace recharge fees are left out of this base because their rates already include the
  service centre's overhead (see "Cost basis").
- **Tuition:** added outside the F&A base, because MTDC excludes it.

**7. Fixed versus marginal cost.**
`fixed = reference phases × (DFT cost + MLIP cost if used)`; `fixed per output = fixed / outputs`.
Entering a new chemistry can require computing the known phases that make up the convex hull, once, before any
candidate can be judged. That one-off cost is spread over the outputs of the batch, so it halves when the batch
doubles (a test checks this). Marginal cost is everything else. **The base reference-phase count is 0**: we
reuse the MP hull, as Szymanski & Bartel do. A sourced count is still missing.

**8. Dollars.**
`$ = core-hours × price per core-hour + GPU-hours × price per GPU-hour + synthesis $`.
All compute stays in hours until this step, so switching the price basis (on-demand or spot, one switch) changes
only this line. GPU-hours appear only for MatterGen generation, priced at the A100 rate.

**9. The three outputs.** Each is split into compute dollars and synthesis dollars per output.
Level 1: `compute $ / correct DFT-stable verdicts`.
Level 2: `(compute $ + synthesis $) / targets made`, where `made = verdicts × success`. (1 - w) is on the
compute term, not here, because success is unconditional.
Level 3 (lower bound): `the same spend / new materials`, where
`new = made × (share of MP-novel candidates also absent from Alexandria and ICSD) × (new-to-science share)`.
Each level divides the total spent up to that point by the number of things you have at that point. The first
novelty share is 62.8% / 72.4% = 0.867 for ion exchange and 0.906 for MatterGen. The second is the Wilson upper
bound on 0 of 36 (see "Level 3").

**10. Break-even synthesis multiplier.**
Pipelines B and C get success × m. Their level-2 cost falls by the factor m (the same spend, m times the output),
while A's is unchanged. `src/analysis.py` finds the smallest m that makes the largest level-2 cost reach a chosen
multiple of the smallest. With A the most expensive, that is m = target × (cheapest of B, C) / A.

## Correlations and named scenarios (used by the stage-2 Monte Carlo)

`params/parameters.yaml` has a `correlations` block. Its entries are parameters that must not be sampled
independently:

- **Recipes per target and synthesis success** (negative). Failed targets use up the recipe budget, so high
  success with many recipes is not physical.
- **Pool stability, filter purity and novelty for one method** (same experiment). They should be drawn jointly,
  ideally by bootstrapping Szymanski & Bartel's published per-structure results.
- **Recall and purity** (constrained). The implied pass fraction must be ≤ 1.
- **Synthesis success, novelty and wrong-verdict share** (one worldview). These come from the same contested
  re-analyses, so they are handled by scenarios, not independent draws.
- **CPU and GPU price basis**, and **the labour role** (salary, fringe, tuition and throughput belong to one
  person).

The `scenarios` block defines three coherent readings of the contested synthesis evidence. Stage 2 will report
them next to the Monte Carlo, because a reader can argue with a worldview but cannot argue with a percentile.

| `middle_prein` (Prein et al. 2025) | 29.2% (7/24) | 16% | 9.6% |

## Modelling decisions

1. **Candidate pools.** A and B screen an ion-exchange (substitution) pool, built with the data-mined
   ionic-substitution model of Hautier et al. (2011) [`hautier2011substitution`]. C generates with MatterGen. Every
   pipeline pays its own generation cost, and structures already in MP are dropped before any calculation.
2. **Synthesis success is unconditional, as each campaign reports it.** It is NOT conditioned on a correct DFT
   verdict: the campaign-observed rate already embeds whatever computational failure that campaign experienced,
   so applying (1 - w) as well would charge it twice. High: A-Lab 36/57 = 63.2%, including the three targets
   that failed for computational reasons. Base: Prein et al. 7/24 = 29.2%. Low: Leeman et al. 3/58 = 5.2%.
3. **Stable** means at or below the MP hull (strict). The 100 meV/atom figures are recorded for stage 2.
4. **The labour pairing is a graduate trainee with trainee throughput.** Postdoc salaries are not used, because
   no source gives postdoc throughput.
5. **Cost basis `funder`:** indirect cost at the 42% average effective indirect cost recovery rate (58% negotiated
   is the high end) on direct costs except tuition and
   service-centre fees. `bench` and fees-inside-base variants are printed alongside.

## Limitations

1. **Primary threat to validity: synthesis success is assumed independent of pipeline.** Nothing in our sources
   measures this, and the expected direction makes 1.17× a lower bound on the level-2 spread. See the section
   above.
2. **MLIP filter recall is not measured on these pools.** Purity (measured) and recall (Matbench) come from
   different sources and cannot both be exactly right.
3. **Finding (b)'s size depends on a figure-read value.** MatterGen's 3.8% purity is read from Supplementary
   Fig. 13. Across its range, C/B at level 1 spans 2.2–6.7×; the direction does not change.
4. **F&A treatment is an accounting choice.** Between `bench` and the default it moves level 2 by 37%
   ($744 against $1,019 for A), but not the spread (see "Cost basis").
5. **Level 3 rests on one disputed count (0 of 36)** and is reported only as a lower bound.
6. **DFT cost spans more than two orders of magnitude.** Results are reported across the span, not at one point.
7. **Wrong-verdict share is a proxy:** flips under a higher level of theory, not failures against experiment.
8. **Precursor mass per attempt is unsourced for A-Lab.** The 1.5 g base is from one manual study. Precursors
   are 1.0% of an attempt at base (1.4% with their indirect cost). With the high mass and the dearest mix (5 g at
   $9.39/g), they are 14% (20% with indirect cost).
9. **Stage-2 choices that shape the Monte Carlo.** The three readings of the synthesis evidence get equal weight
   (an assumption; the named scenarios are reported so readers can apply their own). DFT cost is log-uniform over
   3–1,000 core-h. Draws violating the recall–purity constraint are rejected. The tornado is one-at-a-time and
   ranks evidence gaps as much as sensitivities.
10. **The DAF gap has an unresolved competing explanation.** The two papers judge stability against different
   hulls (MP only against MP plus generated candidates).
11. **Facility recharge rates understate the true resource cost of XRD and furnace time.** Strom et al. (2020,
   *MRS Bulletin* 45(5), 331-335) [`strom2020`] report that user-fee income alone does not fully support most shared
   instrumentation facilities. Our XRD ($80 per sample) and furnace fees are those
   recharge rates, so they are lower bounds on resource cost. This makes the synthesis cost conservative, and a
   higher synthesis cost would widen the gap between synthesis and compute, which strengthens the conclusion rather
   than weakening it. (The claim is taken from the article as summarised; its text could not be fetched to quote, so
   check it before submission.)

## Open gaps

- `fixed.reference_phases_per_chemistry` = 0 (missing). Fixed cost is therefore 0 in the base case.
- **Crucible firing life** (10–100, base 20) is an assumption. The base is a proxy from industrial saggars.
- **Other consumables** omit plastic vials, glovebox consumables and agate wear. These items are covered only by
  the high value ($25).
- **The TiSiN₂ and BaTiS₃ precursor routes are cost proxies** (no TiN or TiS₂ price).
- **The MatterGen filter purity (3.8%) is read from a plot,** Supplementary Fig. 13; the text gives only "3.8% to
  8.8%". It is graded `figure_read`, and its range is widened for plot-reading error.
- **Literature numbers need checking by eye before publication.** Those from `sources/` and from 2026-10-10
  lookups were read through web-fetch summaries. Two Fisher prices were spot-checked against the live pages.
