## The only free lever

**The only free lever moves cost per validated material by 0.7%–2.9%.** Switching compute from on-demand to spot prices is available today at no research cost: 62% off CPU and 15% off A100 time. It changes level-2 cost per validated material by -1.22% (A), -0.74% (B), -2.87% (C) at the base point, with Monte Carlo medians of -0.88% (A), -0.54% (B), -1.98% (C). So the only lever whose cost is known (zero) moves the result by 0.7%–2.9% (under one percent only for B). The levers worth 20–114 times more per percentage point are synthesis success, recipes per target and cost per attempt, and improving any of them requires synthesis research whose cost nobody has measured.

## Discovery acceleration factor

### Named finding: CHGNet's discovery acceleration factor is 1.65× on a realistic ion-exchange pool, against 3.36× on WBM

The discovery acceleration factor (DAF) is the ratio of the filter's precision to the pool's prevalence: how many
times more often a filter-passed candidate is stable than a random one. Matbench Discovery defines it exactly so
("Formally the DAF is the ratio of the precision to the prevalence"). On Szymanski & Bartel's pools it is the
measured post-filter purity divided by the measured unfiltered stable fraction. That is the same quantity on both
sides, so the comparison needs no assumption about which classifier rate transfers.

| Pool | Stable fraction | Purity after CHGNet | DAF | DAF if Matbench rates held at this prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WBM (Matbench Discovery, reported) | 15.3% |  | 3.36× |  |
| ion-exchange pool (Szymanski & Bartel) | 9.2% | 15.2% | **1.65×** | 4.01× |
| MatterGen pool (Szymanski & Bartel) | 3.0% | 3.8% | **1.27×** | 5.05× |

On the ion-exchange pool CHGNet's enrichment is 1.65×. On MatterGen's pool it is
1.27×: the filter barely enriches at all. DAF depends on prevalence (its ceiling is
1 / prevalence), so the last column applies Matbench's recall and false-positive rate at each pool's own
prevalence. That makes the like-for-like comparison 1.65× against
4.01×, a larger gap than against the reported
3.36×.

**Mechanism (rests on an assumption).** If Matbench's recall (0.76) transfers to these pools,
the measured purity implies a false-positive rate of 0.43 on the ion-exchange pool
(0.59 on MatterGen's), against 0.13 on WBM. The other attribution is
that Matbench's false-positive rate transfers and recall collapses. That would need recall of
0.23 and
0.17, which are implausible. The DAF result above does not
depend on this attribution.

**Why the gap: two explanations, one unresolved.**

1. *Distribution shift is weak as an explanation.* WBM was "generated via chemical similarity-based elemental
   substitution of MP source structures" over five rounds (Matbench Discovery, section 2.1.2). That is the same
   construction as Szymanski & Bartel's data-driven ion exchange. If anything, Matbench's unique-prototype subset
   removes structures whose protostructure matches MP, while ion exchange only reuses MP prototypes (0% novel
   prototypes). So the WBM test set is not the more conservative pool.
2. *The stability criteria differ, an unresolved competing explanation.* Matbench labels a structure stable if
   it is "on or below the convex hull of the MP training set" (MP v2022.10.28). It explicitly rejects using the
   joint MP + WBM hull, and it notes that only about 20k of the about 33k WBM structures below the MP hull remain
   on the joint hull. Szymanski & Bartel compute ΔE_d ≤ 0 against phase diagrams that "included all entries from
   MP (as of June 2025) as well as the entries generated from each AI model or baseline approach". That is a
   newer MP and a hull that also contains the other generated candidates, close to the joint-hull formulation
   Matbench rejected. The two criteria agree on the threshold (on or below the hull) but not on the hull. A
   stricter hull lowers both prevalence and the share of passed candidates that count as stable, so part of the
   gap may be definitional rather than a weakness of the filter. Public data can resolve this: recompute CHGNet's
   DAF on WBM against the joint MP + WBM hull. Until then the DAF gap is a measured fact, but its cause is open.


## Marginal returns

Return = % fall in cost per validated material per 1% improvement in the lever: the log-elasticity d ln(cost) / d ln(lever) at the evaluation point, signed so that a positive number means the improvement lowers cost. It is scale-free, so levers in different units compare directly.

| Group | Lever | A base | B base | C base | A MC median (5–95%) | B MC median (5–95%) | C MC median (5–95%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tied, synthesis | synthesis success rate | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) | 1.000 (1.000–1.000) |
| tied, synthesis | recipes per target | 0.980 | 0.988 | 0.953 | 0.986 (0.833–0.999) | 0.991 (0.890–0.999) | 0.968 (0.663–0.998) |
| tied, synthesis | cost per synthesis attempt | 0.980 | 0.988 | 0.953 | 0.986 (0.833–0.999) | 0.991 (0.890–0.999) | 0.968 (0.663–0.998) |
| compute | compute price | 0.020 | 0.012 | 0.047 | 0.014 (0.001–0.167) | 0.009 (0.001–0.110) | 0.032 (0.002–0.337) |
| compute | DFT cost per candidate | 0.020 | 0.012 | 0.046 | 0.014 (0.001–0.167) | 0.009 (0.001–0.110) | 0.032 (0.002–0.337) |
| compute | MLIP precision (filter purity) | 0.000 | 0.012 | 0.046 | 0.000 (0.000–0.000) | 0.009 (0.001–0.110) | 0.032 (0.002–0.337) |

**The synthesis levers are tied at unit elasticity; they are not ranked.** Level-2 synthesis cost is recipes × cost per attempt / ((1 − w) × success), so each of the three has elasticity one by construction. Success is exactly 1 because it also divides the compute term. Recipes and cost per attempt equal the synthesis share of level-2 cost (0.980 for A, 0.988 for B, 0.953 for C). The gap between them is the compute share, not a difference in leverage. `tests/test_stage2.py` asserts this structure.

**The finding is the magnitude gap.** A 1% improvement on the synthesis side lowers cost per validated material by at least 0.98% (A), 0.99% (B), 0.95% (C). The best computational lever lowers it by at most 0.020% (A), 0.012% (B), 0.047% (C). That is a ratio of 50× (A), 82× (B), 20× (C) at the base point, and 70× (A), 114× (B), 30× (C) at the Monte Carlo medians. Per percentage point, synthesis is worth 20–114 times compute, depending on the pipeline and the evaluation point.

- Level 1: B vs A **separates**: B is cheaper than A in 99.5% of draws and they differ by more than 10% in 98%; C vs B **separates**: B is cheaper than C in 99.6% of draws and they differ by more than 10% in 100%.

The expectation going in (level 1 separates, level 2 does not) is confirmed in a specific sense. At level 2 the ordering B < A still holds draw by draw, because the synthesis term is shared and the compute term is not, but the difference is immaterial. **The marginal distributions overlap heavily at every level, level 1 included (B–A overlap 89%).** The absolute cost is dominated by uncertainty that all pipelines share: the 3–1,000 core-h DFT span at level 1, and the synthesis worldview at level 2. So the pipelines cannot be told apart by comparing their absolute cost ranges. Only the paired comparison, the same world under each pipeline, separates them, and only at level 1.

## Break-evens

**1. Screening scale at which the MLIP filter's fixed cost pays for itself: a non-result.** No such scale exists: there is no batch size at which the filter fails to pay for itself. B's extra fixed cost over A is loading CHGNet once (0.86 s, first-hand) plus relaxing the reference phases with it (0 at base; the count is still missing), 0.00024 core-h in all. The filter saves 29.5 core-h ($1.32) on every MP-novel candidate, so the first candidate repays it 123,503 times over. The informative quantity is the per-candidate saving: any other fixed cost X core-h, such as fine-tuning, pays back after X / 29.5 candidates.

**2. Synthesis cost per attempt below which compute starts to matter for level 2.** Compute 'matters' here means it is at least 10% of a pipeline's level-2 cost, or the level-2 spread reaches 1.10×.

**3. MLIP purity at which C becomes competitive.** C matches B at level 1 when CHGNet's purity on MatterGen's pool reaches 15.5%, 4.1× the measured 3.8%. Equivalently, enrichment on that pool would need to be 5.2× instead of 1.27×. C matches A (no filter, ion-exchange pool) at 9.3%, 2.5× the measured value. Parity at level 1 implies parity at level 2, because the synthesis term is shared. The purity cannot fall below 2.28% (prevalence × recall).

## Tornado (summary)

**Read this tornado as a map of where the evidence is weakest, not of where the leverage is.** One parameter carries almost all of the one-at-a-time variance at each level: `dft.cost_per_candidate` (99.8% at level 1, B) and `synth.success_given_correct_label` (98.8% at level 2, B). They dominate because they have the widest ranges (3–1,000 core-h; 5–70% success), not because the cost responds more strongly to them. For leverage per unit change, see the marginal returns, where three synthesis levers are tied at unit elasticity. The two quantities the paper most needs better measurements of are DFT cost per candidate on modern hardware and synthesis success on independent campaigns.
